Biography

A career across disciplines and decades.

From gamma distribution theory to global oncology trials.

Statistics gave me a seat at tables I would never otherwise have reached: reproductive medicine, neonatal pathology, tribal health, climate change and cultural heritage, Raman spectroscopy of erythrocytes, fibromyalgia sleep profiles. I did not plan a career that crosses this many boundaries. It happened because I kept saying yes to people whose questions were more interesting than my own comfort with a method.

The foundation was mathematical: a doctorate on gamma distribution theory and supervised learning at Pondicherry University, where I developed heuristic approaches for handling skewed data that intersect with what the world now calls machine learning. An MBA in Quality Management and triple ISO Lead Auditor credentialing (9001, 14001, 45001) added systems thinking. Six years as faculty and Head of Department (in-charge) at Manipal Academy of Higher Education built the teaching, mentoring, and administrative depth.

In November 2025, I moved from academia to industry, joining Eli Lilly and Company, Bengaluru, as Associate Advisor, Statistics (Oncology). The rigour is the same but the stakes feel closer, the timelines are harder, and the work has a directness that academia sometimes lacks. I contribute to Phase II and III clinical trials, statistical analysis plans, regulatory submissions under FDA and EU frameworks, and industry-academia collaborations with universities in India.

Alongside research and practice, I have co-authored over 150 collaborative Scopus-indexed papers, eight edited volumes (including a six-language public health series), a book chapter, and ten registered copyrights. I have led evidence synthesis through more than thirty PROSPERO-registered systematic reviews, and I serve as Statistical Reviewer for the Journal of Advanced Nursing, F1000, Frontiers, and BMC journals. As a teacher I have delivered more than fifty workshops across fifteen cities in India, and as a mentor I have supervised over a hundred master's theses and co-guided eight doctoral scholars.

Reflections

A few honest paragraphs.

On teaching, leadership, and research, what I have come to believe.

On teaching.

I came to teaching the way many statisticians do, by accident. A doctorate that embeds ML to gamma distribution theory (way before the world screamed AI) does not prepare you to stand in front of nursing students who need to understand p-values and why they should care. But I stayed for reasons that had nothing to do with the accident.

I love designing courses. Not delivering them, designing them. Working out what a programme in Digital Epidemiology should contain when no one in the country has built one before. Figuring out what Statistics for Biotherapeutics means when the students are bench scientists, not mathematicians. Writing a certificate course in Health Technology Assessment because someone needed to teach it and I wanted to learn it first. That is the part no one tells you about teaching: the best courses come from subjects you did not fully understand when you agreed to teach them. Causal inference was like that. I said yes, then spent months reading, and the teaching was better for it because I was still close to the confusion.

The classroom itself is translation. Every statistical idea has at least three audiences: the one that wants the proof, the one that wants the intuition, and the one that wants the answer. The craft is recognising which audience is in the room and honouring all three when they sit together. I have taught machine learning to MBBS students reading radiology papers, biostatistics to nurses finishing thesis defences at midnight, and data analytics to public policy students who had never opened R. The method changes every time. The underlying negotiation does not.

But the audience for statistics should not stop at the university gate. I have spoken at an ATL Tinkering Lab at my old school, talked about Pi Day at a government school in Alappuzha, and run workshops in institutions that had never had a statistician visit. If a subject only makes sense to people who already have a degree in it, something has gone wrong with the teaching.

I do not believe knowledge can be assessed in marks on an exam. The reward of teaching is not the lecture and it is not the grade sheet. It is the smile, the particular one, the smile of enthusiasm, not politeness, when something clicks. It is the student who learns something you did not teach, who goes past the syllabus because the syllabus made them curious enough. Their success is my success, and I mean that without sentimentality. The best learning I have witnessed almost always happened in conversations outside classrooms.

On leadership.

I was given administrative responsibility early, not because I did not say no, but because someone saw something in me before I had a name for it. Coordinator of placements and industry collaborations at first, then alumni coordinator, editorial coordinator, social media coordinator for the school, Quality Coordinator for PSPH, Programme Coordinator for MSc Data Science, MSc Digital Epidemiology, and MSc Health Technology Assessments, and eventually Head of Department in-charge. I sat on Boards of Studies, joined doctoral advisory committees for over fifty PhD scholars, co-guided eight doctoral students across medicine, machine learning and digital health, and contributed data analytics to the university's THE and QS ranking submissions. Each role came because someone recognised a capacity to work with people, not just manage them.

What I have learned is that academic leadership is mostly invisible. It is showing up to committees that have no agenda, reading drafts on weekends, sitting through doctoral defences having studied every detail of the work being presented, and absorbing the small bruises so that the work continues. But the invisible part is not the hard part. The hard part is remembering that you are not above the people you represent. You are part of them. I love working with people. I see a leader in every member of my team. I learn from them. I grow because of them.

Somewhere along the way, I also became a quality person. Lean Six Sigma Black Belt, CQI/IRCA Lead Auditor in ISO 9001, ISO 14001, and ISO 45001. That is an unusual path for a statistician, but it never felt like a detour. Quality management, at its core, is the same discipline as good research: measure honestly, document what you find, and do not look away when the numbers are uncomfortable. It taught me that leadership is not only about people. It is also about systems, and about having the patience to improve them incrementally when no one is watching.

The Young Leadership Development Programme at MAHE in 2023 gave me language for what I had been doing without language for it. Leadership in academia is not charisma. It is patience, a willingness to be wrong in front of people you respect, and the quiet discipline of showing up reliably for years. Victory can never happen alone.

On research.

I have over 150 papers. Not all of them mattered equally, and I have made peace with that. The ones that mattered were almost never the ones that were strategically chosen. They came out of conversations, with a clinician puzzled by a dataset, a doctoral student stuck on a method, a colleague who had read something interesting. The Generative AI ethics paper started as a coffee conversation. The Ayushman Bharat health policy book started with a student's frustration about how health schemes are communicated to the people who need them most, and ended up published in six languages. The 30+ PROSPERO protocols started because evidence synthesis became, for me, a kind of meditation: read everything, record honestly, reduce to what is true.

Some of the work I value most is not the kind that gets cited. I have built instruments: questionnaires, scales, assessment tools. The Manipal Inventory of Academic Stress, the Manipal Inventory of Depression, Anxiety and Stress, the Death Literacy and End-of-life Awareness Preparedness questionnaire, adaptations and validations of tools for autism, occupational stress, uterine fibroid quality of life. Ten registered copyrights so far. Building a questionnaire is a different kind of research. You are not analysing someone else's data. You are constructing the thing that will generate the data, and every word in every item carries weight. It is painstaking, often invisible, and I find it deeply satisfying.

What I have come to believe is that research is most useful when it sits at the boundary between two disciplines that do not usually talk. The work I am most proud of crosses statistics and clinical medicine, statistics and reproductive science, statistics and public policy, statistics and ethics. I have worked on sperm cryopreservation and Raman spectroscopy of erythrocytes, on school mental health interventions in Nepal and tribal health awareness in coastal Karnataka, on neonatal autopsy methods and fibromyalgia sleep profiles. I did not plan a career that looks like that. It happened because I kept saying yes to people whose questions were more interesting than my own comfort with a method. Research gave me something no university could: the chance to learn subjects deeply that I would never be eligible to take a degree in. I will never hold an advanced medical degree, but I have sat inside the questions that medicine asks, and statistics gave me a seat at that table.

And I have never stopped being a student myself. Diplomas in mental health and wellness, mindfulness practitioner certification, a postgraduate programme in health economics, another in epidemiology that I am still pursuing, a certification in environment impact assessment. None of these were required for any job I held. I pursued them because the questions I was encountering in research kept pulling me into territories where my existing training was not enough, and I would rather learn properly than pretend to know.

In November 2025, I moved from academia to industry, joining Eli Lilly as a statistician on oncology clinical trials. It is a different world. The rigour is the same but the stakes feel closer, the timelines are harder, and the work has a directness that academia sometimes lacks. I do not yet know what this chapter will teach me. But I know the pattern by now: say yes to the question, learn what you need to learn, and trust that the mathematics will hold.

The mathematics is the easy part. The hard part is listening long enough to understand what someone actually needs from the numbers. The papers I care about most are the ones where the question came first and the technique followed, not the other way around.
